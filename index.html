Countries like Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, and Singapore have become offshore computing hubs for Chinese companies, such as Alibaba and ByteDance, allowing them to access powerful Nvidia chips via remote access to data centers, thereby circumventing U.S. restrictions. This strategy has attracted billions of dollars in investment to the region. However, U.S. officials are increasingly viewing these data centers as a workaround that poses economic and national security risks, leading to proposals for tighter controls. The new regulations could jeopardize these investments and force regional nations to strike a more difficult balance between Washington and Beijing. The situation is further complicated by the recent resignation of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, linked to an affair with a woman identified by U.S. intelligence as a Chinese intelligence asset who operated a Singapore-based data center company allegedly involved in smuggling chips to China. The news contributed to a broader decline in tech stocks, with significant drops seen in Alibaba and Tencent shares in Hong Kong.

关注点 The U.S. move to tighten AI chip export controls in Southeast Asia reflects a broader geopolitical strategy to curb China's technological advancement. It highlights the critical role of intermediary countries and data centers in the global AI supply chain and the challenges faced by nations in navigating U.S.-China tech competition. The potential impact on billions of dollars in investment and the delicate regional balance underscores the significant economic and political ramifications of these U.S. policies.
