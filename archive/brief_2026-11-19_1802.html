A song chosen to welcome Sanae Takaichi at the APEC summit in Shenzhen grew out of protests against the American military presence in Japan. As they arrived for a banquet in Shenzhen, China, on Wednesday, world leaders were welcomed with fanfares by Chinese musicians. They played “America the Beautiful” for President Trump, the folk song “Cielito Lindo” for President Claudia Sheinbaum of Mexico and the traditional ballad “Drifting Ferns, Floating Clouds” for Vietnam’s leader, To Lam. When Japan’s prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, entered the hall, the band offered a more unconventional choice: “Ue o Muite Arukou,” also known as “Sukiyaki.” The song, which was released in 1961 and became a global hit, was inspired partly by protests against the U.S. military presence in Japan after World War II. Was China, which has been locked in a bitter political feud with Japan for a year, trying to send a musical message to Ms. Takaichi, a defense hawk and close American ally? “It’s only natural to suspect that the choice of song was carefully choreographed,” said Ken Moriyasu, a senior fellow at the Hudson Institute in Washington and a former foreign correspondent for Nikkei. The point, he added, was clear: “A song rooted in opposition to the U.S.-Japan alliance, being played to welcome a Japanese prime minister in China.” China has been at odds with Japan since last year, when Ms. Takaichi suggested that Japan could be forced to get involved militarily in case of a Chinese attack on Taiwan.
